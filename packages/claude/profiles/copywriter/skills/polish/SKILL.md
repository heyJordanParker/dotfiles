# Polish

The parts are written. The headline lands, the sections each make their point, the call-to-action is sharp. What's missing is the connective tissue between them — the reader hits the end of one section and the start of the next with a jolt, because nothing carries them across. Polish is the last pass before the gate clears: it makes the seams disappear so the piece reads as one argument moving in one direction, not a stack of good blocks.

Polish never adds an argument, a claim, or a proof point — the writer did that already. It works only on the joins.

## What a seam needs

A reader crosses a section break carrying momentum. The break either keeps that momentum or spends it. A good transition does three things at once:

- **Closes the prior beat** so the reader feels the point landed, not abandoned.
- **Opens a question the next beat answers** so the reader has a reason to keep going.
- **Holds the through-line** so every section feels like the same argument, not a new one.

A seam fails when the next section restates what the reader already knows, when it jumps to a new idea with no bridge, or when it drops the momentum and makes the reader start over.

## The common joins

Each structure has the same handful of seams every time. Bridge them on their own terms.

- **Problem → agitate → solve.** The agitation inherits the exact problem just named, not a broader one; the solution answers the agitation's sharpest line, not the original problem in general. The reader should feel the temperature rise, then break.
- **Story → lesson → call-to-action.** The lesson falls out of the story's specific ending, never arrives as a separate claim; the call-to-action is the lesson made actionable, the obvious next move for someone who just believed it.
- **Feature → benefit → proof.** The benefit is the feature's outcome in the reader's terms; the proof backs the exact benefit just promised, placed where the doubt spikes.
- **Section → section down a long page.** Each section ends pointing at the next — a line that closes its own point and leans forward. The page reads as one descent, never a list.

## How to bridge

- **Carry a word.** End a section on a charged word and open the next on it, or its echo. The repetition is the handrail the reader holds across the gap.
- **Ask the question the next section answers.** A one-line question at a seam turns a break into a pull.
- **Cut the throat-clearing.** Most rough seams are a section that opens by re-introducing its own topic. Delete the warm-up; start on the point and the join tightens itself.
- **Match the energy.** A flat sentence after a sharp one reads as a stall. Keep the cadence moving across the break, then vary it inside the next section.
- **Never bridge with filler.** "Furthermore", "in addition", "that being said", "but here's the thing" are the absence of a transition, not a transition. If the only bridge you can find is a connective adverb, the two sections aren't actually in sequence — reorder them or cut one.

## What you return

The piece with its transitions smoothed, and the seams that were rough named — the section break, what jolted, the bridge you made. You touch only the joins and the throat-clearing around them. If a section's content itself is the problem — a missing claim, a weak point, an unbacked promise — that's a strategy or line failure, not a seam. Flag it; don't paper over it with a smoother transition.
