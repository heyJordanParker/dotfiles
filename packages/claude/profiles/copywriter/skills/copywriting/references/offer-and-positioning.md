# Offer and Positioning

Copy sells whatever is underneath it. A clear page on a weak offer converts worse than a rough page on a strong one. Before you write, settle three things: the one promise, the position against what the reader would otherwise pick, and the offer itself. If any of the three is wrong, stop and flag it — words will not fix it.

## The one promise

One asset, one transformation. The promise is the single before-to-after the reader buys: where they are now, where they'll be, in their terms. Not a feature, not the category — the outcome they'd repeat to a colleague.

- **Surface ask vs real outcome.** The reader asks for the deliverable; they want what it gets them. "I want analytics" is "I want to know which features to build next." Name the real outcome — it's almost always one or two levels above the ask, and usually emotional or identity-based.
- **The test.** Can the reader picture the morning after this works, in concrete sensory terms? If they can repeat the promise back to a friend, it's specific enough. If they can't, it's still a category, not a promise.

## Positioning: the wedge against the named alternative

The reader is never choosing in a vacuum. They are choosing between you and something — a competitor, a spreadsheet, a manual process, or doing nothing. You are positioned against that alternative whether you name it or not, so name it and win on one axis.

- **Frame of reference** — what category the reader files you under, so they know what you replace. ("A lifecycle email tool" tells them what shelf you're on.)
- **Point of difference** — the one axis where you beat the named alternative. One, not five. The wedge is sharper when it's single.
- **Reason to believe** — why the difference is real and not a claim. This is where proof attaches (see proof-and-credibility.md).

> Weak: "The best scheduling tool for teams." (No alternative named, no axis.)
> Strong: "Calendly books whoever grabs the slot first. We route each meeting to the rep who can actually close it — so a hot lead never lands on the wrong desk." (Names the alternative, picks one axis, implies the mechanism.)

The most common SaaS alternative is not a competitor — it's the status quo (doing it by hand, or not doing it). Position against the status quo by making its cost vivid, then showing the wedge.

## Choosing the battlefield: category and best-fit customer

The wedge above wins the fight you're in. These two levers choose the fight — and they move conversion more than any sentence you write, because they decide who the reader compares you to and whether the page is aimed at them at all. A founder controls both, and most pages waste them by defaulting to the obvious category and writing for everyone.

- **Market-category choice** — the category you claim sets your competitors, the reader's reference price, and what they expect for the money. Pick it; don't inherit it. Compete in a category where your strength is the table-stakes norm and your weakness doesn't count, or frame a category of one where there's nothing to compare you to. The same product files differently depending on the shelf you put it on, and the shelf decides the comparison.

  > "Another email tool" lands you next to Mailchimp on price and feature count. "The lifecycle revenue engine for solo founders" is a shelf with no incumbent — different competitor, different price ceiling, different proof required.

  The cost of a new category is education: if no one is shopping for it yet, you pay to teach the market it exists before you can sell inside it. Reframe an existing category when you can; create one only when the existing shelf guarantees a loss.

- **Best-fit-customer selection** — you do not write for everyone; you pick the one reader the product fits best and aim every lever at them. Narrowing the reader sharpens all the others at once: the promise gets concrete, the named alternative gets obvious, the proof becomes people visibly like them, and the "my situation is different" objection (proof-and-credibility.md) shrinks because the page is visibly about their situation. "For SaaS teams" converts worse than "for the solo founder running trials by hand" — not because the market is smaller, but because the second reader feels seen and the first feels handed a brochure.

  Choosing a best-fit customer is not abandoning the rest of the market; it's choosing whom the page speaks to so it speaks to anyone at all. Aim narrow on the page even when you sell broad.

## The offer

The offer is the value the reader weighs against the price. They buy when perceived value beats price; they walk when it doesn't, no matter how good the copy. Four levers move perceived value — multiply the top, shrink the bottom.

```
            Dream outcome  ×  Perceived likelihood of getting it
  Value  =  ───────────────────────────────────────────────────
            Time to result   ×   Effort and sacrifice
```

- **Dream outcome (raise).** Name the specific result, not the generic one. "Invoices that chase themselves" beats "better billing." The bigger the reader's pain, the more this matters — they're buying a future, not a feature.
- **Perceived likelihood (raise).** Do they believe it'll work *for them*? This is the most underweighted lever. Most stuck offers are fine on the dream and starved on belief. Raise it with proof, a named mechanism, a guarantee, and people like them who got the result — not with more features. **When copy isn't converting, this is usually the gap.**
- **Time to result (shrink).** How long from buying to the first win. Engineer a fast first win and say when it lands. "See your first invoice reconciled in 10 minutes" beats "powerful automation." Slow results don't just feel slow — they erode belief that it works at all.
- **Effort and sacrifice (shrink).** What the reader pays besides money — setup, learning curve, decisions, risk. Done-for-you beats do-it-yourself. Fewer decisions, smart defaults, "import in one click." Founders especially pay in time and attention; remove those costs and the offer jumps.

### For self-serve SaaS, pricing does the offer work

A SaaS offer is not a bonus stack. The offer *is* the packaging, the value metric, the trial, and the price. That's where you raise value and lower friction.

- **Value metric** — charge along the axis the reader's value grows on (per seat, per contact, per project, per usage). The right metric makes the price feel fair because it scales with what they get. The wrong metric makes every plan feel like a tax.
- **Packaging** — three tiers, the middle one built to be chosen, the recommended plan marked. More than four tiers is decision paralysis (see psychology.md). Each tier names who it's for, not just what it includes.
- **Trial or free tier** — the trial is risk reversal. "Free for 14 days, no credit card" lowers effort and raises belief at once. A free tier lets the reader own the product before paying (the endowment effect, psychology.md).
- **Why now** — the reason to act today. For SaaS this is rarely fake scarcity; it's founding-member pricing, a price increase coming, or the compounding cost of waiting ("every week without this is another cohort of trials that churned"). Real, or leave it out.

## Diagnostic: when the page won't convert

Score each lever 1-10, honestly. The lowest is the binding constraint — fix that before touching anything else.

- Dream outcome: can they picture the after, and repeat it back?
- Perceived likelihood: do they have three named, comparable proof points? Is the mechanism specific enough to repeat? Have you answered "but my situation is different"?
- Time to result: what's the first win, and how soon?
- Effort and sacrifice: how much work, how many decisions, how much risk?

If the lowest lever is perceived likelihood — and it usually is — the fix is proof and mechanism, not a bigger claim or a lower price. Most "we need to lower the price" conversations are "we have a value lever starved" conversations.
