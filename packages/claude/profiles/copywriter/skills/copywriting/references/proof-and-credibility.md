# Proof and Credibility

Every claim either earns belief or spends it. Proof is what makes a claim cost nothing — it raises perceived likelihood, the most underweighted lever in the offer (see offer-and-positioning.md). When a page won't convert and the offer is sound, the gap is almost always proof, not promise. The fix is more evidence, not a bigger claim.

Use only the proof in the brief. Never invent a number, a quote, or a customer — a fabricated proof point that gets caught torches every real one with it.

## Pair every claim with proof

The unit of persuasion is the claim-proof pair. A claim alone is an assertion the reader is free to doubt; a claim with proof attached is a fact. Write them together — make the claim, then immediately pay for it.

> Claim alone: "Cut your reporting time dramatically."
> Claim + proof: "Cut your reporting time by 80% — that's the 4 hours a week [customer]'s ops team got back."

Place proof at the two moments doubt spikes: right after a benefit claim, and right beside the CTA where the reader decides.

## Specificity is the proof

Specific beats glossy every time. A precise, slightly awkward number reads as true; a round, polished one reads as marketing.

- Numbers with decimals and context beat round numbers. "11.3% trial-to-paid, up from 4%" beats "3x conversion."
- Named customers beat anonymous ones. "[name], Head of Growth at [customer]" beats "a marketing leader."
- Concrete detail beats adjectives. "Saved 4 hours every Monday" beats "saves tons of time."

## Types of proof, strongest first

- **Case studies** — problem → solution → result, with named customer and real metrics. The heaviest proof; one detailed case beats ten logos.
- **Specific testimonials** — quotes with a result, a before/after, a name, a role, a company, and ideally a face. (More on selection below.)
- **Data and demonstrations** — show the product doing the thing, or the aggregate result across customers. Showing beats claiming.
- **Logos** — recognizable customers, placed near the top. Recognition matters more than count; five known names beat fifty unknown.
- **Stats** — "10,000+ teams," "4.9/5 across 1,200 reviews." Builds credibility cheaply; weakest alone.
- **Authority and media** — expert endorsement, certifications, "featured in." Borrows trust the reader already grants.

## When you have no case study yet

A pre-revenue founder has none of the proof above — no case study, no logos, no stats, no testimonials. That is the default starting point for this work, not a failure, and "go get a case study" is useless advice to someone with zero customers. Mining which proof you can credibly produce is research's job; this section is the output side — what to put on the page instead, and in what order. Each type below stands in for the missing case study by answering the same question a different way: *why should I believe this works?*

Ranked by how much belief they buy, strongest first:

- **Live demonstration** — show the product doing the thing, on screen, start to finish. A case study reports that it worked for someone; a demonstration lets the reader watch it work right now, which they trust more because they're verifying it themselves rather than taking your word. The closest substitute there is — a recorded run, an interactive sandbox, a "watch it build the report" clip placed right beside the claim.
- **Proof of mechanism** — when you can't show that it worked for someone, show *why* it must. Name the mechanism and make it followable: the specific thing the product does that the old way can't. A reader who understands why it works needs fewer examples that it did. This is the reason-to-believe lever from offer-and-positioning.md carrying the whole load alone.
- **Founder credibility and origin** — why you built this and what you know that the reader doesn't: the years in the problem, the scar that made you build it, the unfair insight. This borrows the authority slot (see Types above) when there's no customer authority to borrow yet — the reader trusts the maker because they can't yet trust the made.
- **Building in public** — real numbers, even small ones, shown openly: signups this week, what shipped, what's next. Momentum and transparency substitute for outcome proof by showing the thing is alive and moving toward the reader's result. Weakest alone and slowest to pay off, but it manufactures the raw material — first users, first results — that becomes every stronger proof on the page above.

The honesty rule bites hardest here, where the temptation is highest: a small real number beats a big invented one, and one true demonstration beats a page of borrowed credibility. Never dress a hypothesis as a result. As real proof arrives — the first user, the first metric — it replaces these substitutes from the bottom up.

## Selecting testimonials

The best testimonials do work; the worst take up space.

- **Use:** specific results ("increased trial conversion by 32%"), before/after context ("we used to send one welcome email and pray"), role and company for credibility, something quotable and concrete.
- **Cut:** "Great product!", "Love it!", "Easy to use!" — praise with no result attached proves nothing and signals you had nothing better.

Match the testimonial to the reader. A reader who'll doubt "this won't work for my situation" needs a testimonial from someone visibly in their situation — same role, same size, same problem. This answers the universal "my situation is different" objection — others get results, but I'm the exception — more directly than any claim can.

## Risk reversal

Risk reversal raises perceived likelihood and lowers effort at once — it tells the reader you'll only offer this if you're sure, and it removes the cost of being wrong.

- **Guarantees** — money-back, results-based, or "you don't pay until it works." The right one depends on refund risk; a specific, conditional guarantee reads as confident, a vague "100% guaranteed!" reads as unenforceable.
- **Free trial / free tier** — "Free for 14 days, no credit card." The lowest-friction risk reversal in SaaS; it lets the reader prove it themselves.
- **Cancel anytime** — removes the fear of being trapped, the quiet objection behind most subscription hesitation.

Place the risk reversal next to the CTA, where the reader is weighing the downside of acting.

## Trust placement on the page

- **Logo or stat bar** high, just under the hero — borrows credibility before the first real claim.
- **A proof point after each benefit section** — the claim-proof pair, repeated down the page.
- **Testimonials beside decision points** — near the pricing, near every CTA.
- **Risk reversal at the CTA** — the last doubt, answered where it's felt.
- **Objection handling (FAQ, comparison) late** — for the reader still hunting reasons not to act after the case is made.
