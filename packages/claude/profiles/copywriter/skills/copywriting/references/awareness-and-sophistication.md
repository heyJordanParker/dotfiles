# Awareness and Sophistication

The single most important decision in copy is made before a word is written: where the reader stands, and where the market stands. Two axes, both from Eugene Schwartz's *Breakthrough Advertising*. Awareness is about the **reader** — how much they already know. Sophistication is about the **market** — how many times they've heard the claim. Get both right and an ordinary headline converts; get them wrong and a brilliant one bounces.

This file is the one home for the awareness model in the whole profile. Everything else — the lead, the headline, the proof, the length — is downstream of what you decide here.

## The five stages of awareness (the reader)

A reader can only be moved one step at a time. Copy that skips a stage talks past them. Name the stage, then write to it.

1. **Unaware.** They don't know they have the problem, or won't admit it. You cannot sell the product or even the problem head-on. Enter through their world — a story, a startling fact, an identity they hold. The headline names *them*, not you. Longest copy; you build the problem before you reveal a solution exists.
   > "Most founders think their churn is a product problem. It's a first-week problem."

2. **Problem-aware.** They feel the pain but don't know solutions exist, or don't know they're solvable. Lead with the problem, agitate the cost of leaving it, then reveal that a category of solution exists. The headline names the problem in their words.
   > "Your onboarding emails are why trials don't convert."

3. **Solution-aware.** They know the kind of solution exists; they don't know yours, or why it's better. Lead with the promise and the mechanism — the specific how that makes the result believable. The headline names the result plus the path.
   > "Turn trial signups into paying customers with lifecycle emails that trigger on what users actually do."

4. **Product-aware.** They know your product; they're not convinced. Lead with the offer, the differentiation, the proof, the risk reversal. The headline names the product and its edge over the named alternative.
   > "The only lifecycle tool that writes the emails for you — not just sends them."

5. **Most aware.** They know it and want it; they need a reason to act now. Lead with the offer, the price, the deal, the CTA. The headline names the terms.
   > "Founding price ends Friday — lock in $29/mo for life."

**The progression rule.** As awareness rises, three things happen together: the lead moves problem → solution → product → offer, the copy gets shorter, and the proof shifts from "this problem is real" to "this product is the one." Match the most advanced thing the reader already believes, then take them exactly one step further.

## The five levels of market sophistication (the market)

Sophistication is not about the reader's intelligence — it's about how worn-out the claims in this market already are. Each level burns out the last and forces the next move.

1. **Level 1 — first to market.** No one has made this claim. State it plainly and directly; the claim itself is the headline. "Lose weight." You only get this once, and almost never in a crowded SaaS category.

2. **Level 2 — second wave.** Competitors echo the claim. Enlarge it — bigger, faster, more complete. Push the same claim to its credible limit. "Lose 30 pounds in 30 days."

3. **Level 3 — claims exhausted.** Bigger claims are no longer believed. Introduce a **new mechanism** — the unique *how* that makes the claim possible again. The mechanism restores belief that the worn-out claim couldn't. "The thermogenic protocol that burns fat while you sleep."

4. **Level 4 — mechanism war.** Competitors copy your mechanism. Enlarge the mechanism — make yours superior, more elegant, faster, with fewer downsides. "The second-generation protocol — 3x faster, no stimulants."

5. **Level 5 — market exhausted.** No claim and no mechanism is believed; the market is cynical. Shift to identity and connection. Sell to who the reader is, lead with the reader themselves, win on brand, affinity, and felt experience. "For the founder who's tried every growth hack and is done being sold to."

**The response rule.** The level you're at dictates the move: state the claim → enlarge the claim → introduce a mechanism → enlarge the mechanism → become the identity. Diagnose by counting: how many competitors make your exact claim, and how tired does the reader sound? Most B2B SaaS categories sit at level 2-3 — the claim is heard, the mechanism is not yet exhausted, so a named, specific mechanism is usually the winning move.

## Message-to-market match

Response collapses to one question: does the message match the market's state of mind on arrival? The match has three parts, and all three must hold.

- **Match the awareness stage.** A most-aware reader hit with a problem-aware lead feels condescended to and leaves; an unaware reader hit with an offer-led page has no idea why they'd care. The headline's job is to land exactly where the reader already is.
- **Match the sophistication level.** A bare claim in a level-3 market reads as a lie. A heavy mechanism pitch to a level-1 market is noise. Pitch at the level the market has reached, not one behind or ahead.
- **Match the prior message.** Whatever sent the reader here — an ad, a search result, an email subject — set an expectation. The page must continue that exact promise in the same words. Break the continuity and the reader bounces before reading. (An ad promising "cut reporting time" must land on a page that opens on reporting time, not on a generic homepage.)

The match test, before you ship: read the headline as the reader who just arrived from the actual source. Does it name what they already believe, in the words they'd use, continuing what they were just promised? If any of the three is off, the headline is wrong no matter how good it sounds.

## The response matrix

What each awareness stage demands, end to end. Use it to sanity-check that the whole piece is aimed at one stage.

- **Unaware** — Lead: story or identity. Headline: names the reader's world. Proof: that the problem is real and costs them. CTA: soft, a next read, not a buy.
- **Problem-aware** — Lead: the problem, agitated. Headline: names the pain. Proof: you understand it better than they can say it. CTA: learn how it's solved.
- **Solution-aware** — Lead: promise + mechanism. Headline: result plus the path. Proof: the mechanism works, and works for people like them. CTA: see it, try it.
- **Product-aware** — Lead: offer + differentiation. Headline: product plus its edge. Proof: comparisons, named results, risk reversal. CTA: start trial, buy.
- **Most aware** — Lead: the deal. Headline: the terms. Proof: a reason to act now (real scarcity or urgency). CTA: hard, buy now.
