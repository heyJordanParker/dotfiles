# Conversion Pages

Every page is a different job. A landing page argues for one action; a sales page closes a paid purchase in one long scroll; an optin page trades a free thing for an email. Each has a recipe, a typical awareness stage, and a characteristic failure. Find the page you're writing, build to its recipe, then run the draft back through the writing principles and the editing skill.

## The section catalog

Pages are built from these blocks. Pick the ones the argument needs, in the order the argument moves — not all of them, every time.

- **Hero** — headline, subheadline, primary CTA, supporting visual. The whole pitch in one screen.
- **Social proof bar** — logos, a key metric, or a star rating, just under the hero. Borrows credibility early.
- **Problem section** — articulate the pain better than the reader can. Create recognition, hint at the cost of leaving it.
- **Solution / benefits** — 3-5 benefits, each a headline + one line + proof. Not ten; the list dilutes.
- **How it works** — 3-4 numbered steps, each an action and its outcome. Reduces perceived complexity.
- **Testimonials / case studies** — proof at decision points (see proof-and-credibility.md).
- **Use cases / "built for"** — lets the reader self-identify ("for marketers who…").
- **Comparison** — against the named alternative or the status quo.
- **FAQ** — the late objections, answered.
- **Final CTA** — recap the promise, repeat the action, attach the risk reversal.

## Page assets

### Landing page

- **Job:** complete one argument for one action, on one page, for traffic from one source.
- **Recipe:** Hero → social proof bar → problem → how it works (3 steps) → 2-3 benefits → testimonial → comparison → FAQ → final CTA with risk reversal. Compress for ad traffic: Hero → proof bar → 3 benefits → testimonial → how it works → final CTA.
- **Stage:** match the ad or source — usually problem-aware or solution-aware.
- **Moves:** message-match the headline to the exact promise that sent them (see awareness-and-sophistication.md); one CTA, repeated; strip the nav so the only path is forward.
- **Mistake:** a feature list instead of an argument. A landing page is a single line of reasoning, not a brochure.

### Sales page

- **Job:** close a paid purchase in a single long scroll. Where the landing page completes one argument for one action — compact, usually earning a trial — the sales page runs the full emotional arc and does the price-justification, value-framing, and risk-reversal the landing page only gestures at. The landing page argues; the sales page closes.
- **Recipe:** Hero (promise + subhead at the reader's stage; lead with a product demo (scripted as a Demo / VSL, below), a before-after, or the dream outcome in words, never dependent on an image) → picture-the-after ("Imagine…", the vivid sensory dream outcome) → problem agitated (end on the reader's limiting belief) → mechanism reveal ("But what if…", the named mechanism, honest if it's real) → solution intro (the product *as* the mechanism; show the access shape — the plan or the trial) → what it does for them (Feature-Benefit Bridge, outcome-first, see persuasion-structures.md) → proof after each benefit and beside each CTA (see proof-and-credibility.md) → price anchor against a real number (annual-vs-monthly, a competitor's cost, or the cost of inaction — never an invented struck-through price) → offer + price box + outcome-named CTA → risk reversal at the CTA (free trial, no card, cancel anytime, or a results guarantee) → value framing that shows what the plan really includes, anchored to the real alternative cost (not a bonus stack with invented retail values — a SaaS offer is not a bonus stack) → why-now that is real (a founding price that genuinely ends, a dated increase, the compounding cost of waiting) → FAQ (the late SaaS objections: setup, migration, security, commitment, refund, integrations) → closing CTA (recap the promise, repeat the ask, re-attach the risk reversal) → legal footer.
- **Stage:** solution-aware → product-aware, and warmer traffic — your list, a launch, retargeting, not cold ad clicks. Governing structure: PASTOR or 4Ps end to end (see persuasion-structures.md).
- **Moves:** ask several times, not once — repeat the CTA at each decision point, each beside fresh proof and value; lead every benefit with the outcome and let the feature follow as the reason it's real; keep every anchor, deadline, and mechanism the honest version, because one caught exaggeration discredits every true claim above it.
- **Mistake:** borrowing a course template's fake scarcity and invented value-stack instead of doing the real offer work. Fabricated urgency or a made-up anchor gets caught, and torches every real claim on the page.

### Demo / VSL script

- **Job:** the spoken script behind the demo or video sales letter the sales page hero leads with — earn the first ten seconds, carry the page's argument aloud, and hand the viewer to the page's CTA. The Sales page entry above is the written recipe; this is the script craft that fills its demo slot.
- **Recipe:** open on the stakes (a hook that buys the first ten seconds — the result, the sharp pain, or the question that names it, never a name-and-logo intro) → problem-to-product (state the pain the viewer already feels, then turn it on the named mechanism — the spoken PAS or PASTOR arc, see persuasion-structures.md) → show, don't tell (screen-record the product doing the one job that proves the promise; narrate the outcome over the action, never read the interface) → let the proof happen on screen (the number moving, the task finishing — a demonstration is proof, see proof-and-credibility.md) → hand-off to the CTA (name the next click and match the page's outcome-named CTA word for word).
- **Stage:** the stage of the page it sits on — solution-aware to product-aware; the viewer already pressed play, so open on the payoff, not the setup.
- **Moves:** pick one job and show it end to end — the single flow that proves the promise, never a tour of the menu; write the open last, once the demo has shown you its strongest ten seconds; keep every on-screen result real — record the product actually producing it, because a staged result torches the page's proof the same way a fake anchor does; speak the page's exact words so the video and the page read as one argument, not two.
- **Mistake:** a feature tour — walking the interface panel by panel with no job getting done. The viewer came to see the product solve their problem, not to watch a menu get clicked.

### Optin page

- **Job:** trade one specific, instantly-useful free thing for an email and do nothing else — grow the list, don't sell the product.
- **Recipe:** compressed Hero (headline = the lead magnet plus its one benefit) → mockup of the deliverable → 3-5 bullets of what they get → single-field form (email only) with a benefit-CTA → one line of social proof. No nav, no footer, no product pitch.
- **Stage:** problem-aware or solution-aware, not ready to evaluate software; message-match the magnet to the traffic source (see awareness-and-sophistication.md).
- **Moves:** one field beats two; the CTA says what they get ("Send me the teardown"), never "Submit"; promise instant gratification — a quick win the moment they open it; strip every exit so the only move is the trade.
- **Mistake:** selling the product instead of the free thing, or asking for name, company, and role before the reader has received anything.

### Homepage

- **Job:** serve several audiences without going generic, and route each to their next step.
- **Recipe:** Hero on the broadest true promise → proof bar → segmented paths (by role or use case) → 2-3 flagship benefits → testimonials → final CTA.
- **Stage:** mixed; write the hero for the least-aware visitor, the paths for the more-aware.
- **Moves:** lead with the one outcome that's true for everyone, then branch. Give the "ready to buy" and the "still researching" reader each a clear next click.
- **Mistake:** averaging every audience into a promise so broad it means nothing.

### Pricing page

- **Job:** help the reader choose the right plan and reduce the anxiety of choosing wrong.
- **Recipe:** plan comparison with the recommended plan marked → annual/monthly toggle → per-plan "best for {who}" line → value-anchored framing → FAQ on billing and limits → guarantee / "cancel anytime."
- **Stage:** product-aware to most-aware; they're close.
- **Moves:** three tiers, middle one built to win (see psychology.md on anchoring and choice); add an enterprise or "contact us" tier above them as the high anchor that makes the middle tier feel reasonable; default the toggle to annual and frame the discount as the saving, not just a smaller number ("2 months free"); name who each plan is for; answer "which is right for me?" before they ask.
- **Mistake:** listing features without telling the reader which plan is theirs.

### Feature page

- **Job:** connect one feature to the outcome and the reason to try it.
- **Recipe:** Hero on the outcome the feature delivers → Feature-Benefit Bridge (see persuasion-structures.md) → use cases → demo or screenshot → CTA to try.
- **Stage:** solution-aware to product-aware.
- **Moves:** lead with what the feature *gets* the reader, let the feature appear as the reason it's real; show it working.
- **Mistake:** describing the feature's mechanics with no outcome attached.

### About page

- **Job:** tell why you exist, in a way that connects the mission to the reader's benefit.
- **Recipe:** the origin / why-it-exists → what you believe → how that belief serves the reader → CTA (yes, an about page converts).
- **Stage:** any; often the skeptic checking if you're real.
- **Moves:** a real story differentiates from faceless competitors; tie the mission to the reader's outcome, not your pride.
- **Mistake:** a company timeline nobody asked for, with no CTA.

### Upsell page

- **Job:** lift average order value with one relevant, time-boxed offer in the window where trust and buying momentum peak — after payment, before the thank-you page.
- **Recipe:** Hero framing an exclusive, now-only add-on → one line on why it complements what they just bought → one proof point → real urgency → single one-click accept plus a plain decline.
- **Stage:** most-aware — they just paid; open on the offer and the terms, skip the long build (see persuasion-structures.md).
- **Moves:** one click with no re-entered payment — the non-negotiable that makes the offer work; ride the momentum ("add it to the order you just placed"); keep the offer genuinely relevant — an annual upgrade, a seat pack, a premium add-on, a complementary tool; cap at one or two, never a stack.
- **Mistake:** an unrelated offer, any added friction (re-asking for the card), or stacking three upsells until the buyer regrets the first purchase.
