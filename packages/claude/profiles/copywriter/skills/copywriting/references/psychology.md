# Copy Psychology

The cognitive models that change a line of copy. The full marketing-psychology canon runs to fifty models; most govern strategy, channels, or pricing structure. These are the ones that decide a word, a frame, or an order on the page. Each names the lever and the copy move it dictates. Reach for the one that fits the moment — never stack them, or the copy reads as manipulation.

## Belief and proof

- **Social proof** — people do what others like them do. Show counts, named customers, reviews. "Join 4,000 founders" beats "join us." (See proof-and-credibility.md.)
- **Authority** — readers defer to credible sources. Lead a claim with the expert, the credential, or the "featured in" that backs it.
- **Concreteness (availability)** — vivid, specific examples feel more true and more likely than abstractions. One named customer's morning beats a statistic about many.
- **Cognitive fluency** — copy that's easy to read is judged more true. Short words, short sentences, familiar phrasing. Friction reads as doubt.
- **Curse of knowledge** — you can't unsee what you know; your product feels obvious to you and opaque to a newcomer. Write for the reader who's never heard of your category, and test the copy on one.
- **Pratfall effect** — admitting a real flaw raises trust. "We're not the cheapest" before "here's why we're worth it" makes the rest believable.

## Desire and framing

- **Loss aversion** — losing hurts about twice as much as gaining feels good. Frame the cost of not acting, not only the upside of acting. "Stop losing trials" pulls harder than "get more conversions."
- **Framing effect** — the same fact lands differently by frame. "90% of trials stay" beats "10% churn." Choose the frame that points at the reader's desire.
- **Contrast** — value is judged against what it's set beside. Show the before-state vividly so the after looks like the obvious escape.
- **Anchoring** — the first number sets the scale. Show the higher price, the competitor's cost, or the cost of doing nothing before your price.
- **Mental accounting** — the same price feels different reframed. "$1 a day" lands lighter than "$30 a month"; "less than one churned customer" reframes the spend as a saving.
- **Zero-price effect** — free is not just cheap, it's a different category of decision. "Free for 14 days" pulls disproportionately; lead with it when it's true.
- **Hyperbolic discounting** — readers prefer a small reward now to a large one later. Promise the immediate win. "See your first result today" beats "ROI within six months."

## Action and friction

- **Paradox of choice** — too many options paralyze. One clear primary CTA. Three pricing tiers, not seven. Every extra choice is a reason to leave.
- **Commitment and consistency** — a small yes makes the next yes easier. Ask for the low-commitment step first (the free trial, the one field), then build.
- **Zeigarnik effect** — open loops hold attention until closed. A headline that opens a question, a "you're 80% done" — the unfinished pulls the reader forward.
- **Goal-gradient** — effort accelerates near the finish. Show progress ("2 of 3 steps done") to drive completion in onboarding and forms.
- **Regret aversion** — readers avoid choices they might regret. The guarantee, the "cancel anytime," the "no credit card" remove the regret that blocks the click.

## Identity and connection

- **Mimetic desire** — people want what desirable others want. Show that the people the reader admires already use this. Waitlists and "trusted by" trigger it.
- **Unity** — shared identity persuades harder than logic. "Built by founders, for founders" signals one-of-us. Use the reader's own in-group language. (The level-5 sophistication move; see awareness-and-sophistication.md.)
- **Reciprocity** — give first and the reader wants to return it. A genuinely useful free tool, guide, or tier creates the pull before you ask for anything.
